Your next train, on the wall

The six slots

A fixed three-column grid. Where you put a card is where it stays.

The board is three columns wide and at most two cards tall. Slots are numbered down each column, so card 1 and card 2 share the left column, 3 and 4 the middle, 5 and 6 the right.

Card 3middle, top
Card 5
Card 2left, bottom
Card 4middle, bottom

Card 6 is set to Off here, so card 5 takes the whole right column — and roughly twice the departures.

Leaving a slot Off is how you get a tall card. The remaining card in that column stretches to fill it and shows about twice as many rows. A column with both slots off disappears and the others widen.

Setting up a card

Six fields. Only the station is required.

Show this cardcardN_visible

Untick to park a card without losing its settings — a summer ferry, a station you only use on weekdays. Its column-mate then fills the column and shows about twice as many departures.

Station or stopcardN_station

Type at least two characters and pick from the list. One search covers all five networks — there is no transit type to choose first — and each result says which it is: Jamaica (E J Z) · Subway beside Jamaica · LIRR.

You never type a stop ID. The search resolves the hard cases for you: a complex comes through with every platform, a bus corner with both sides of the street.

RoutescardN_routes

Optional. Comma-separated — 1,2,3, and spaces around the commas are fine. Blank means everything that calls there.

Works on every card type: subway lines, railroad branches (Babylon, Hudson), bus routes (M42) and ferry routes (ER).

See Choosing routes, which is also how you split a crowded station across two cards.

DirectioncardN_direction

Optional. Blank shows both directions side by side; pick 1 or 2 and that one gets the whole card, which gives destinations room instead of clipping them into two narrow columns. Worth doing when you only ride one way from a stop.

On a railroad, 1 is towards the city and 2 is away from it. On subway and bus, 1 and 2 are the card's left and right columns; the heading above each says where it goes.

Ignored on a ferry card, which has no inbound and outbound to split.

Walk timecardN_walk

Minutes from your door to the platform. Departures you could not reach in time are hidden, so the first row is always one you can still make.

Measure it honestly, stairs included — everything else on the card depends on this number.

Card headingcardN_title

Optional. Defaults to the station name. Worth setting when the default is long, or when you'd rather read Home than Vernon Blvd-Jackson Av.

Display settings

Four settings apply to the whole board:

  • 24-hour clock — off by default.
  • Highlight threshold — rows this close to your leave time are drawn white on black. Default 5 minutes.
  • Destination text size — smaller shows more of a long station name.
  • Leave in N minutes — adds a countdown column. Off by default, because the panel only refreshes every 15 minutes, so a countdown can be stale where a clock time never is.
Refresh rate

Set 15 minutes or slower — that is TRMNL's minimum for store plugins.

Choosing routes

Use the letter or number painted on the train.

Leave Routes blank and the card shows everything that stops there. Fill it in and the card narrows. Write the lines the way you'd say them out loud, comma-separated:

Three ways to narrow a card

Just the local
1,2,3
One line
G
A shuttle
S

Two conveniences are built in, because the feeds name things differently than signs do:

  • Express is included automatically. Asking for 7 gives you the 7 and the 7 — the same line, running express. You never write 7X.
  • Every shuttle is S. One entry covers all three — 42nd St, Franklin Av, and Rockaway Park — because that is what each one is signed as. The board sorts out which is which.

The Staten Island Railway is SI, matching the bullet on the new R211 trains rather than the SIR you may remember from the subway map.

Railroads, buses and ferries too

The same field narrows any card. Use the name on the badge — a railroad branch, not a destination:

Capitalisation does not matter, and a partial name works — far finds the Far Rockaway branch. Matching is generous, so M4 brings in the M42 along with the M4; write the full route name when you want just the one.

Branch, not destination

Filter a railroad card on the branch — Babylon — not on where the train ends up, which is often somewhere else along it.

Splitting a big station

Times Square is twelve routes. You probably want two of them.

Pick Times Sq-42 St and you get one card carrying the 1, 2, 3, 7, N, Q, R, W, A, C, E and the shuttle — twelve routes competing for the same handful of rows, with the trains you actually take buried among them.

Use the same station on two cards, with a different route filter on each.

Card 1

left column, top

Station: Times Sq-42 St
Routes: 1,2,3

Card 2

left column, bottom

Station: Times Sq-42 St
Routes: N,Q,R,W

One card is now the red lines and the other the yellow, each with its own heading and its own share of the rows. The same approach works anywhere a complex carries more than you care about — Atlantic Av-Barclays Ctr, Fulton St, Jay St-MetroTech.

Splitting by direction instead

The same trick splits a station by direction rather than by route, which is the better choice when you take one line both ways. Point both rows of a column at the same station, set Direction to 1 on one and 2 on the other, and name them Downtown and Uptown. Each card then shows one direction at full width.

Card 1

left column, top

Station: 116 St-Columbia University
Direction: 1
Card heading: Downtown

Card 2

Set a card heading when you split

Both cards default to the station's name, so you get two identical headings. Name them — Times Sq 1/2/3 and Times Sq N/Q/R/W.

If the heading says Port Authority

Times Square's A/C/E platform is officially 42 St-Port Authority Bus Terminal, part of the same complex through the same passageways. The search offers all of it under Times Sq, so that heading is the station doing the confusing, not the board. Rename the card if it bothers you.

Railroads & ferry

One list per card. On a railroad, an arrow for direction.

LIRR, Metro-North and ferry cards all read the same way: a route badge, where the train or boat is headed, and the time. One list, no direction columns.

Jamaica LIRR
Port Jeff‹ Penn Station11:25
Ronkonkoma› Ronkonkoma11:23
Babylon› Massapequa11:29

The badge is the branch — Port Jefferson, Ronkonkoma, Babylon on the LIRR; Hudson, Harlem, New Haven on Metro-North. The text beside it is where that particular train ends up, which is often not the branch's namesake.

Inbound and outbound

The arrows

‹ is toward the city — Penn Station, Grand Central, Atlantic Terminal, Long Island City, Hunterspoint Av.
› is away from it, out toward Long Island or up the Hudson.

At a through station like Jamaica or Harlem-125 St, trains run both ways from the same platforms, so two rows can carry the same branch badge going opposite directions. The arrow is what tells them apart — check it before you run.

Terminals

At a terminal — Penn Station, Grand Central, Atlantic Terminal — trains only leave in one direction, so a terminal card shows every arrow pointing the same way and gives that direction the card's full width. The branch badge will often repeat the destination: at Penn Station roughly three rows in four read Ronkonkoma › Ronkonkoma, because most trains run to their branch's namesake. All of that is correct.

Leave Direction blank at a terminal

Nothing departs Grand Central towards the city, so a card there filtered to direction 1 is empty. Leave it blank, or pick 2.

This card shows fewer trains than the station's board

The boards inside a terminal list arrivals as well as departures; this card lists only trains you can board. If you are meeting someone off a train, read the station's board.

Two Grand Centrals

Both railroads serve a station called Grand Central, and they are different places on different tracks — Metro-North upstairs, the LIRR in Grand Central Madison deep below. The search lists both, so read the network on the end of the result: Grand Central · Metro-North or Grand Central · LIRR. Pick the wrong one and you get real trains going somewhere you did not mean.

The ferry

NYC Ferry works the same way: pick a landing, get one list. The badge is the route code — ER East River, SB South Brooklyn, SG St. George, RS Rockaway, AS Astoria — and the destination is the operator's own headsign.

Wall St/Pier 11 Ferry
EREast 34th Street10:58
ASEast 90th St11:26

Ferry rows carry no arrow and the Direction setting does nothing on a ferry card. NYC Ferry routes are not radial the way a commuter railroad is — the Rockaway route runs south and east — so there is no inbound and outbound to label. The headsign says where the boat is going.

Ferries stop running earlier than trains

Service is roughly 5am to 10pm. Outside those hours a ferry card is empty because no boats are sailing.

Bus directions

A bus stop is one side of the street.

The key fact

A bus stop is one side of the street. The pole across the road is a different stop with a different ID — same name, opposite direction.

A subway station has trains running both ways from one place; a bus stop does not. So a card built on a single stop would show one direction with no sign that the other exists.

The search handles this for you. Look up a corner and it is offered once, carrying both sides — pick it and the card renders two columns, one per direction.

BORDEN AV/5 ST
Q67LONG ISLAND CITY14:08
Q67MIDDLE VILLAGE14:15

Bus columns carry no heading — one stop serves several routes going different places, so each row names its own destination. Those names run longer than the column is wide and will cut off mid-word.

If you only see one direction

You picked a single stop rather than the corner. Search again and choose the entry naming the cross-street — that is the one carrying both sides.

About stop IDs

You never type one, but three things about them explain odd behaviour.

Behind every station name is one or more stop IDs — 721 for Vernon Blvd-Jackson Av, 505507 for one side of a bus corner. The search fills them in for you. Three things about them explain behaviour that is otherwise baffling.

One station can be several IDs

Times Square is five separate records sharing a complex: the 1/2/3 platforms, the 7, the shuttle, the N/Q/R/W, and the A/C/E. The search offers all five as one choice, so you get the whole complex and narrow it with Routes.

One ID can be several lines

Coney Island-Stillwell Av is a single ID carrying the D F N Q. If you want fewer, that is what the line filter is for.

Bus stop IDs expire

The MTA retires and re-numbers bus stops. The pole stays on the corner but its ID starts returning an error, so a bus card that worked for a year can go bad on its own. The search only offers stops that are currently valid — re-pick the stop and it works again.

If a bus card says "No such stop"

That is a retired ID. Open the card, search the corner again, and pick it fresh. The name will usually look familiar with the cross-streets swapped.

Reading the board

What the shapes, tags and highlighting mean.

7 Vernon Blvd-Jackson Av
734 St-Hudson Yards14:08
734 St-Hudson Yards14:13
  • A circle is a local — it makes every stop.
  • A diamond is the same line running express, the way the MTA signs it.
  • A highlighted row, white on black, means leave now. The threshold counts from your leave time, not the departure — a highlighted row is one where walking out the door is already overdue.
  • Two columns on a subway card are the two directions, labelled with the MTA's own platform wording — Manhattan, Queens — rather than north and south.
  • A tag beside the heading — LIRR, Metro North, Bus, Ferry — names the network. Subway cards show route bullets there instead.
  • An arrow on a railroad row is the direction of travel: ‹ toward the city, › away from it.
  • One column where you expected two means a terminal: nothing is boardable the other way, so the live direction takes the full width.

When a card looks wrong

Most surprises turn out to be the transit network, not a bug.

Card is empty

The feed had nothing to show, which is not the same as a failure. Check your walk time first — a generous one hides everything in the next quarter hour. Late at night, service genuinely thins out.

Every train goes the same way

Your station is a terminal: trains only leave in one direction from the end of a line. See Terminals.

Wrong Grand Central

LIRR and Metro-North both serve one. Re-pick the station and read the network after the · — Grand Central · LIRR is the deep Madison level, · Metro-North is upstairs.

Only one direction

On a bus card, you picked one side of the street — see Bus directions. On a subway card at a terminal, this is correct: nothing is boardable in the other direction.

A card you configured is missing

Its station could not be resolved — usually a value saved before a change to the picker. Open the card and re-pick the station.

A line is missing

Check Routes — if it is filled in, anything unlisted is hidden by design. Clear it to see everything the station carries.

Card says "Unavailable"

That card's feed failed, and only that card is affected — the rest of the board keeps working. Usually it resolves itself by the next refresh.

Times look hours off

The board renders in New York time regardless of where your panel is. If every time is wrong by the same amount, that is worth reporting.

Every row comes from a live feed. The board never falls back to a printed timetable, so if a train is not running you will not see it — which is the point, but it does mean an empty card is sometimes just an accurate one.

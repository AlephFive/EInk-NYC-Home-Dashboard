TRMNL Recipe · Setup Guide

Your next train, on the wall

Six cards of live departures on a TRMNL panel — subway, LIRR, Metro-North, bus and NYC Ferry. This guide covers setting up each card, splitting a complex like Times Square, and the places where NYC transit is stranger than it looks: bus directions, railroad terminals, and stop IDs.

The six slots

A fixed three-column grid. Where you put a card is where it stays.

The board is three columns wide and at most two cards tall. Slots are numbered down each column, so card 1 and card 2 share the left column, 3 and 4 the middle, 5 and 6 the right.

Card 1left, top
Card 3middle, top
Card 5
Card 2left, bottom
Card 4middle, bottom

Card 6 is set to Off here, so card 5 takes the whole right column — and roughly twice the departures.

Leaving a slot Off is how you get a tall card. The remaining card in that column stretches to fill it and shows about twice as many rows. A column with both slots off simply disappears and the others widen.

Three columns is the maximum by design. At four, a subway destination gets about one character of width before it clips.

Setting up a card

Six fields per card, five of them optional.

Show this cardcardN_visible

Untick to park a card without losing its settings — a summer ferry, a station you only use on weekdays. Its column-mate then fills the column and shows about twice as many departures.

Clearing the station does the same thing, destructively.

Station or stopcardN_station

Type at least two characters and pick from the list. One search covers all five networks — there is no transit type to choose first — and each result says which it is: Jamaica (E J Z) · Subway beside Jamaica (LIRR) · LIRR.

You never type a stop ID. The search resolves the hard cases for you: a complex comes through with every platform, a bus corner with both sides of the street.

RoutescardN_routes

Optional. Comma-separated — 1,2,3, and spaces around the commas are fine. Blank means everything that calls there.

Works on every card type: subway lines, railroad branches (Babylon, Hudson), bus routes (M42) and ferry routes (ER).

See Choosing lines, which is also how you split a big station across two cards.

DirectioncardN_direction

Optional. Blank shows both directions side by side; pick 1 or 2 and that one gets the whole card.

Worth doing when you only ever ride one way from a stop — those rows then get full width, so destinations have room instead of being clipped into two narrow columns.

On a railroad, 1 is towards the city and 2 is away from it. On subway and bus, 1 and 2 are the card's left and right columns; the heading above each says where it goes.

Ignored on a ferry card. A landing has no inbound and outbound — the Rockaway route runs south and east — so the operator does not label one and neither does this.

Walk timecardN_walk

Minutes from your door to the platform. Departures you could not physically reach are hidden, so the first row is always one you can still make.

This is the setting that makes the board more useful than the MTA's own app. Measure it honestly — including stairs.

Card headingcardN_title

Optional. Defaults to the station name. Worth setting when the default is long, or when you'd rather read Home than Vernon Blvd-Jackson Av.

Display settings

Four settings apply to the whole board: a 24-hour clock toggle; the highlight threshold (rows this close to your leave time are drawn white on black, default 5 minutes); destination text size, where smaller shows more of a long station name; and "leave in N minutes", which is off by default — the panel refreshes on an interval, so a countdown can be stale while a clock time never is.

Refresh rate

Set 15 minutes or slower. That is TRMNL's store minimum, and it is why the board shows clock times rather than counting down.

Choosing routes

Use the letter or number painted on the train.

Leave Routes blank and the card shows everything that stops there. Fill it in and the card narrows. Write the lines the way you'd say them out loud, comma-separated:

Three ways to narrow a card

Just the local
1,2,3
One line
G
A shuttle
S

Two conveniences are built in, because the feeds name things differently than signs do:

  • Express is included automatically. Asking for 7 gives you the 7 and the 7 — the same line, running express. You never write 7X.
  • Every shuttle is S. One entry covers all three — 42nd St, Franklin Av, and Rockaway Park — because that is what each one is signed as. The board sorts out which is which.

The Staten Island Railway is SI, matching the bullet on the new R211 trains rather than the SIR you may remember from the subway map.

Railroads, buses and ferries too

The same field narrows any card. Use the name on the badge — a railroad branch, not a destination:

Babylon
Two Metro-North lines
Hudson,Harlem

Capitalisation does not matter, and a partial name works — far finds the Far Rockaway branch. Short entries are generous rather than strict: M4 matches the M42 as well as the M4, which is deliberate. A filter that shows a few extra rows is easier to spot and fix than one that empties the card.

Branch, not destination

On a railroad card the badge is the branch — Port Jefferson, Ronkonkoma — and the text beside it is where that particular train ends up, which is often somewhere else entirely. Filter on the branch.

Splitting a big station

Times Square is twelve routes. You probably want two of them.

Pick Times Sq-42 St and you get one card carrying the 1, 2, 3, 7, N, Q, R, W, A, C, E and the shuttle. That is a complete and almost useless card: twelve routes competing for the same rows, and the trains you actually take buried among them.

The fix is to use the same station twice, with a different line filter on each. Nothing special is required — just two cards.

Card 1

left column, top

Station: Times Sq-42 St
Routes: 1,2,3

Card 2

left column, bottom

Station: Times Sq-42 St
Routes: N,Q,R,W

Now one card is the red lines and the other the yellow, stacked in a single column, each with its own heading and its own share of the rows. The same approach works anywhere a complex carries more than you care about — Atlantic Av-Barclays Ctr, Fulton St, Jay St-MetroTech.

Splitting by direction instead

The same trick splits a station by direction rather than by route, which is the better choice when you take one line both ways. Point both rows of a column at the same station, set Direction to 1 on one and 2 on the other, and name them Downtown and Uptown. Each card then shows one direction at full width.

Card 1

left column, top

Station: 116 St-Columbia University
Direction: 1
Card heading: Downtown

Card 2

left column, bottom

Station: 116 St-Columbia University
Direction: 2
Card heading: Uptown

Why the card heading matters here

Two cards from one station will both default to the same heading. Set Card heading on at least one — Times Sq 1/2/3 and Times Sq N/Q/R/W — or you will have two identically-titled cards.

One oddity worth knowing

Times Square's A/C/E platform is officially named 42 St-Port Authority Bus Terminal. It is part of the same complex and you reach it through the same passageways, so the search offers all of it under Times Sq — but if the heading says Port Authority, that is why, and nothing is wrong.

Railroads & ferry

One list per card, with an arrow for direction.

LIRR, Metro-North and ferry cards all read the same way: a route badge, where the boat or train is headed, and the time. No direction columns — unlike the subway, these networks do not run two ways from a single platform in a way worth splitting a card over.

Jamaica LIRR
Port Jeff‹ Penn Station11:25
Ronkonkoma› Ronkonkoma11:23
Babylon› Massapequa11:29

The badge is the branch — Port Jefferson, Ronkonkoma, Babylon on the LIRR; Hudson, Harlem, New Haven on Metro-North. The text beside it is where that particular train ends up, which is often not the branch's namesake.

Inbound and outbound

The arrows

‹ is toward the city — Penn Station, Grand Central, Atlantic Terminal, Long Island City, Hunterspoint Av.
› is away from it, out toward Long Island or up the Hudson.

This matters more than it sounds. At a station like Jamaica or Harlem-125 St, trains run in both directions from the same platforms, and two rows can carry the same branch badge going opposite ways. The arrow is what tells them apart.

Every row you see carries an arrow. A train with nowhere further to go has no direction to report — but it is also not one you can board, so it never reaches the list in the first place.

Terminals

At a terminal — Penn Station, Grand Central, Atlantic Terminal — trains only leave in one direction, because there is no further to go. The board handles this: it shows only trains you can actually board, so arriving trains ending their run are left out, and the single live direction gets the card's full width rather than half a card reading "no upcoming trains".

So a terminal card is correct and complete. It will simply show every arrow pointing the same way, and the branch badge will often repeat the destination — at Penn Station roughly three rows in four read Ronkonkoma › Ronkonkoma, because most trains run to their branch's namesake.

Why the board may look emptier than the station's

The departure boards inside a terminal list arrivals too. This card does not — an arriving train is not one you can catch. If you are meeting someone off a train, the station's own board is the right one to read.

Direction 1 is empty at a terminal

A train counts as city-bound when it ends at a city terminal — so nothing departs Grand Central towards the city, and a card there filtered to direction 1 is correctly empty. Leave it blank, or pick 2.

If your station is a terminal, use it. If you are choosing between a terminal and a nearby through station — Penn Station versus Jamaica, say — the through station packs more into the same rows: ten branches instead of one direction's worth, and destinations that differ from the badge beside them.

LIRR and Metro-North are searched separately

Pick the right Transit type first. Both railroads serve a station called Grand Central and both have a stop numbered 1, but they are different places on different tracks — Metro-North upstairs, the LIRR in Grand Central Madison deep below. Searching the wrong one finds real trains going somewhere you did not mean.

The ferry

NYC Ferry works the same way: pick a landing, get one list. The badge is the route code — ER East River, SB South Brooklyn, SG St. George, RS Rockaway, AS Astoria — and the destination is the operator's own headsign.

Wall St/Pier 11 Ferry
EREast 34th Street10:58
ASEast 90th St11:26

Ferry rows carry no arrow. The routes are not radial the way a commuter railroad is — the Rockaway route runs south and east — so inbound and outbound would be labels the operator itself does not use. The headsign already says where the boat is going.

Ferries stop running earlier than trains

Service is roughly 5am to 10pm. Outside those hours a ferry card is empty, and correctly so — the feed is reporting that no boats are sailing, not failing.

Bus directions

The one place riders are most often surprised.

The key fact

A bus stop is one side of the street. The pole across the road is a different stop with a different ID — same name, opposite direction.

A subway station has trains running both ways from one place. A bus stop does not. If a card showed only one stop, you would see buses going one direction and no hint that the other direction exists.

The station search handles this for you. Search for a corner and it is offered once, carrying both sides. Pick it and the card renders two columns, one per direction.

BORDEN AV/5 ST
Q67LONG ISLAND CITY14:08
Q67MIDDLE VILLAGE14:15

The columns carry no heading, deliberately. A heading could only name one destination, but a single stop serves several routes going different places — so the label would be wrong for most of the column rather than merely redundant. Each row names its own destination instead.

If you only see one direction

You most likely picked a single stop rather than the corner. Re-open the search and choose the entry that names the cross-street — that is the one carrying both sides.

Bus destinations are also longer than the column is wide, so they clip. That is deliberate: an ellipsis costs about three characters of exactly the space being reported as short, and a name cut mid-word still reads.

About stop IDs

You never type one. It still helps to know they are there.

Behind every station name is one or more stop IDs — 721 for Vernon Blvd-Jackson Av, 505507 for one side of a bus corner. The search resolves them, so this section is background rather than instruction. Three things are worth knowing because they explain otherwise-baffling behaviour.

One station can be several IDs

Times Square is five separate records sharing a complex: the 1/2/3 platforms, the 7, the shuttle, the N/Q/R/W, and the A/C/E. The search offers the complex as one choice carrying all five. This is why picking a station by hand elsewhere is risky — choosing one platform group silently gives you real trains, just not the line you meant.

One ID can be several lines

Coney Island-Stillwell Av is a single ID carrying the D F N Q. If you want fewer, that is what the line filter is for.

Bus stop IDs expire

The MTA retires and re-numbers bus stops. A stop that worked last year can stop existing — the pole is still on the corner, but its ID now returns an error. The search only offers stops that are currently valid, so a card configured today will work; a card configured two years ago may need re-picking.

If a bus card says "No such stop"

That is a retired ID. Open the card, search the corner again, and pick it fresh. The name will usually look familiar with the cross-streets swapped.

Reading the board

What the shapes and the inversion mean.

7 Vernon Blvd-Jackson Av
734 St-Hudson Yards14:08
734 St-Hudson Yards14:13
  • A circle is a local — it makes every stop.
  • A diamond is the same line running express. The MTA signs express by shape, so the board does too.
  • A highlighted row (white on black, with rounded corners) means you should be leaving about now. That is counted after your walk time, so it is how long you have before you must go — not how long until the train arrives.
  • Two columns on a subway card are the two directions, labelled with the MTA's own platform wording — Manhattan, Queens — rather than north and south.
  • A tag beside the heading — LIRR, Metro North, Bus, Ferry — names the network. A subway card shows its route bullets in the same place instead.
  • An arrow on a railroad row is the direction of travel: ‹ toward the city, › away from it. See Railroads & ferry.

At a terminal, one direction has nothing you can board: every train there is ending its run. The card drops that column and gives the live direction the full width, rather than showing you half a card that says "no upcoming trains".

When a card looks wrong

Most surprises have a transit explanation rather than a bug.

Card is empty

An empty card means the feed had nothing — not that something failed. Check your walk time first: a generous one filters out everything in the next quarter hour. Late at night, service genuinely thins out.

Every train goes the same way

Your station is a terminal, and that is the whole truth of the place — trains only leave in one direction from the end of a line. Nothing is wrong. See Terminals.

Wrong Grand Central

LIRR and Metro-North both serve one. Check Transit type — the station search looks at one railroad at a time.

Only one direction

On a bus card, you picked one side of the street — see Bus directions. On a subway card at a terminal, this is correct: nothing is boardable in the other direction.

A card you configured is missing

Its station could not be resolved — usually a value saved before a change to the picker. Open the card and re-pick the station.

A line is missing

Check Routes — if it is filled in, anything unlisted is hidden by design. Clear it to see everything the station carries.

Card says "Unavailable"

That card's feed failed, and only that card is affected — the rest of the board keeps working. Usually it resolves itself by the next refresh.

Times look hours off

The board renders in New York time regardless of where your panel is. If every time is wrong by the same amount, that is worth reporting.

The board reports what the live feeds say and nothing more. It never falls back to a printed timetable to guess at a departure, and it never infers why a feed is empty — an empty feed only proves the feed is empty.
